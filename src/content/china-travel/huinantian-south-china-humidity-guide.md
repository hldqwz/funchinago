In late winter and early spring, visitors to Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Foshan or Xiamen sometimes notice something strange: the floor is wet even though it has not rained, the mirror will not clear, and the hotel laundry comes back damp. This is huinantian, and it is one of those local things nobody warns you about.

## Quick Answer

Huinantian (回南天, sometimes called a "south wind sky") is a condensation event, not rain. Warm, moisture-heavy air from the South China Sea moves north over ground, walls and furniture that are still cool from winter. Moisture in the air condenses on those cool surfaces, so walls "sweat", floors become slick, windows stream, and clothes will not dry.

It usually appears from **February to March, and at the latest April**, most often in Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan and Fujian. A single episode commonly lasts **about three to seven days**, though it can persist longer when warm and cool air stay balanced, and the wider season can recur on and off. Some years see little or none of it. There is no fixed start date to put in a guidebook, so plan for the possibility rather than a particular week.

## Why it happens

China Meteorological Administration explainers describe a specific sequence. In winter, cold northern air controls South China. As that cold air weakens in early spring, warm and humid southerly flow from the sea strengthens and pushes back north. When the two air masses are evenly matched, you get days of overcast drizzle. When the warm air wins and advances quickly over still-cold surfaces, the moisture in it condenses exactly the way water beads on a cold glass.

That is the key mental model: the water on the wall came out of the air, not through the wall. It is also why huinantian is hard to forecast precisely. The outcome depends on air temperature and humidity, wind, and the temperature of every different surface indoors, which is why the official explainers say exact prediction remains difficult.

The same sources list the effects locals recognise: water droplets on walls and windows, damp bedding, laundry that never finishes drying, mould on clothes and furniture, short circuits in electrical equipment, and a general feeling of closeness and discomfort. Visibility can also be poor because warm rain and fog often travel with the same air mass.

## How long it lasts — and how it ends

Meteorologists describe two endings. A **cold ending** arrives when a new cold front pushes the humid air away; dry air then evaporates the moisture quickly. A **warm ending** happens when temperatures rise long enough for floors and walls to catch up with the outdoor air, so condensation stops. The warm version takes longer because indoor surfaces heat up slowly.

This distinction is useful for travellers. If a cold front is forecast, the damp may clear within a day. If the forecast is simply "getting warmer", expect a slower, stickier few days. Either way, the official expert guidance is that huinantian in a typical episode lasts around three to seven days, with longer spells possible.

## What to do if you are there

The advice from Chinese weather experts is surprisingly practical:

- **Keep windows shut in the morning and evening**, especially windows facing south or southeast. Those are the humid hours; opening them invites moisture in.
- **Ventilate briefly around midday**, when the air is warmer and relatively drier. A few minutes is enough.
- **Use air conditioning in dry/dehumidify mode, or a dehumidifier.** Hotel rooms with a dry mode are worth asking for when you book.
- **Wipe water off floors and stairs.** Wet tile and stone become genuinely slippery, and this is a common small accident for visitors.
- **Use a tumble dryer or hotel laundry instead of air-drying.** If you are travelling for more than a few days, quick-dry fabrics make the trip much easier.
- **Protect electronics and leather.** Keep them off cold floors and away from windows; a sealed bag with a moisture absorber helps.
- **Give yourself time.** Low cloud and drizzle can slow road and airport movement; check status before a tight connection rather than assuming a clear sky.

None of this makes huinantian dangerous. It makes the trip feel damp and slightly inconvenient, especially if your accommodation has no dryer and your schedule is packed. The visitors who cope best simply adjust: fewer laundry cycles, more indoor afternoons, and flexible plans for the first clear day.

## Where it fits in your trip

Huinantian overlaps an interesting travel window. It is after the Spring Festival rush and before the genuinely hot, typhoon-prone summer, so crowds are manageable and food is excellent. It also coincides with the later flower markets in Guangdong in some years. If you can tolerate a few humid days, the trade-off is reasonable; just do not plan a trip built around long outdoor walks.
