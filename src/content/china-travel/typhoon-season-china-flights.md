A typhoon can turn a well-planned China itinerary into a sequence of cancelled flights and closed attractions in less than a day. The good news is that warnings come early, the transport system recovers quickly, and the refund rules are more traveller-friendly than most people expect — if you know which button to press.

## Quick Answer

Most typhoon activity affecting China comes **between July and September**, with the broader season reaching from late spring into November in some years. The southeast coast — Guangdong, Fujian, Zhejiang, Shanghai and Hainan — is the area most often affected; inland cities such as Beijing, Xi'an and Chengdu are largely outside the path.

China uses a **four-colour warning system**: blue, yellow, orange and red, in increasing severity. Flights may be cancelled because of crosswinds, and coastal high-speed rail sections may be slowed or stopped. If a train is cancelled because of weather, 12306 refunds automatically with no fee. If a flight is cancelled, choose the **involuntary** change or refund channel, not the voluntary one. Add one or two buffer days around coastal stops and keep hotel bookings refundable.

## Reading the warnings

The China Meteorological Administration defines four typhoon warning signals, and the colours are graded by both wind strength and lead time:

- **Blue:** effects possible within 24 hours; average winds around force 6 or gusts around force 8. Prepare and keep watching.
- **Yellow:** effects within 24 hours; average winds around force 8 or gusts around force 10. Large outdoor gatherings stop.
- **Orange:** effects within 12 hours; average winds around force 10 or gusts around force 12. Work, school and large events may stop and people may be moved to safety.
- **Red:** effects within 6 hours; average winds around force 12 or gusts around force 14. This is the highest level and means stay sheltered and follow local orders.

These are the official national thresholds; local authorities may add their own rules. You will see the colours on weather apps, airport screens and hotel notices. For a traveller, blue and yellow are "start adjusting plans", orange is "stop planning outdoor activities", and red is "stay where you are".

## What happens to transport

Flights are the most exposed. A storm does not need to be overhead to matter: crosswinds at an airport can exceed limits while the sky above your hotel looks fine. Airlines typically publish special handling plans for affected routes a day ahead, and cancellations cluster in the six-to-twelve-hour window around the storm's closest approach.

High-speed rail is more resilient but not immune. Coastal lines can be speed-restricted or temporarily suspended in the worst wind sections, and services may resume gradually after the storm passes. Ferries and island boats tend to stop first, sometimes while a typhoon is still hundreds of kilometres away. Metro systems may close elevated or surface sections under the highest warnings. These patterns are reported by transport authorities and travel sources rather than guaranteed by a fixed rule, so always check live status.

## Your rights when plans break

CCTV reported the following rules in August 2026, and they are worth saving:

**Trains.** If weather cancels a train, 12306 automatically refunds the ticket within five days of the original travel date, with no fee. You can also refund at any station window or in the app up to 30 days after the travel date, including that day, again without a fee. One catch: on a connected journey, the system only auto-refunds the segment that was actually cancelled. Other unaffected tickets in the same itinerary need a separate refund request.

**Flights.** Airlines issue special plans for disrupted routes. Eligible tickets — including discounted ones — can generally be refunded in full or changed once for free, though a fare difference may apply. When you apply, select the **involuntary** change/refund channel. If you file it as a voluntary personal reason, you can be charged a fee you did not need to pay.

**Hotel and meals.** For weather-caused cancellations, an airline may help arrange accommodation or meals, but the cost is generally borne by the passenger and there is no automatic cash compensation. That is where travel insurance matters: if you bought a delay or cancellation policy, keep the airline's written proof of the change and claim under the policy terms. Check in advance whether the policy actually covers force majeure events.

## Practical advice

- **Monitor the official maps and your own operators.** The China Meteorological Administration warning page and China Weather's typhoon channel are the primary sources; your airline app and 12306 are the ones that tell you what your ticket will do.
- **Build buffer days.** If you must fly into or out of Shanghai, Guangzhou or a coastal city between July and September, leave 48 hours of flexibility around the connection.
- **Book refundable rooms and keep documentation.** Save the cancellation notice, payment receipt and any messages from staff; you may need them for insurance.
- **Have an inland alternative.** If a coastal stop is threatened, moving inland by high-speed train is often possible before winds become dangerous. Treat any specific route as an example to verify, not a plan to rely on blindly.
- **During the storm, stay inside and away from windows.** Do not step out during the eye: it is a calm interval, and the strongest winds can return abruptly. Charge devices, keep water handy, and follow local evacuation instructions if given.
- **Recheck after the storm.** Services resume quickly but not all at once. Confirm your new departure time before heading to the airport or station.
