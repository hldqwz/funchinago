Guangzhou has a nickname that translates as "City of Flowers", and once a year the name becomes literal. In the days before Lunar New Year, outdoor flower streets appear across the city, selling blossoms, kumquat trees and New Year decorations. They are free, open to everyone, and among the most approachable local traditions a foreign visitor can join.

## Quick Answer

The tradition is called *xing hua jie* — "walking the flower street" — and it is listed as national intangible cultural heritage. Markets are set up by district, usually in the **one to two weeks before Chinese New Year**, with the busiest nights just before New Year's Eve.

For 2026, Guangzhou ran flower markets in all 11 districts, starting on **4 February** and concentrating around **10–17 February**, ahead of Chinese New Year on 17 February. The Yuexiu market on Xihu and Jiaoyu Roads — the historic "century-old" one — ran roughly 13 February into the early hours of 17 February. The Haizhu waterfront market at Canton Tower Plaza West Square ran **4–16 February, 10:00 to 22:00, with free admission**. These are 2026 examples: dates and venues change with the lunar calendar each year, so check the current district announcement before you go.

## What you actually see

A flower market is not a flower show. It is a working street market where families buy plants that symbolise luck and prosperity for the coming year. Kumquat trees heavy with fruit are the classic purchase; orchids, peach blossom, chrysanthemums and arranged baskets sit beside them. Stalls sell red couplets, paper cuttings and calligraphy, and in recent years the bigger markets have added intangible-heritage craft demonstrations, food stalls and evening performances.

The atmosphere is the point. In the early afternoon the pace is calm and families browse slowly. After dark, archways are lit, crowds build, and the market becomes a street festival. The Haizhu waterfront version adds a riverside setting beneath the Canton Tower; the Liwan market has included a "water flower street" with boats; the Tianhe market returns to the Sports Center on the city's central axis. Each district has its own character, which is why locals will tell you there is no single "best" one.

For a visitor, this is unusually accessible. There is no ticket, no reservation, no dress code and no requirement to understand Chinese — you can simply walk, watch and smell the flowers. Vendors are used to curious onlookers, and pointing and smiling go a long way.

The scale is easy to underestimate. The 2026 Haizhu announcement described the market as part of a citywide programme of more than 2,000 Spring Festival cultural events, and Guangzhou alone fitted markets into all 11 districts. The plants are bought for the home rather than as souvenirs: kumquat trees in particular are traditionally associated with luck and prosperity, which is why they appear in almost every photograph. Treat the market as a neighbourhood celebration that happens to be full of flowers, not as a ticketed exhibition, and the experience makes much more sense.

## How to visit well

- **Go in the late afternoon or early evening.** You get the lights and the buzz without the late-night crush. The final night before New Year's Eve is the busiest.
- **Pick one district and stay with it.** The markets are spread across a very large city. Combining two in one evening usually means spending more time on the metro than among the flowers.
- **Bring a payment method that works locally.** Most stalls use WeChat Pay or Alipay; a small amount of cash is a sensible backup. Foreign cards are not something to rely on here.
- **Ask before photographing vendors closely.** Wide street shots are fine; a camera in someone's face is not.
- **Do not buy a tree you cannot carry.** Full-size kumquat trees are meant for local homes. Smaller cut flowers and dried arrangements travel far better.
- **Combine it with a normal Guangzhou day.** The markets sit inside working districts, so pair one with dim sum, a river walk or a visit to the old quarter rather than treating it as a standalone attraction.
- **Recheck the exact location.** Districts move venues between years. The provincial media roundup and each district's official announcements are the sources to trust in January.

## Why it is worth the detour

Most travel writing about Chinese New Year focuses on the transport chaos or the temple fairs of northern China. The flower markets offer a quieter entry point. They are local in the truest sense: people are shopping for their homes, not performing for tourists. They are also free, which makes them easy to add to an itinerary even if the rest of your day changes.
